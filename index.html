CHAPTER-3 🩸 CIRCULATION

Introduction

1. Need for Nutrients, Gases and Liquids
All living organisms need nutrients, gases and liquids for their growth and maintenance. These are essential for keeping the body alive and functioning properly.
2. Transport to All Body Parts
The required materials must be transported to all parts of the body. This transportation is necessary in both unicellular and multicellular organisms.
3. Transport in Unicellular Organisms
In unicellular organisms, materials need to travel only short distances. Therefore, they do not require a complex transport system.
4. Transport in Multicellular Organisms
In multicellular organisms, substances may have to travel very long distances. In the tallest plants, this distance may be around 100 feet.
5. Transport in Amoeba and Hydra
Simple organisms like Amoeba and Hydra transport materials using simple processes — mainly diffusion and osmosis.
6. Diffusion
Diffusion helps substances move without requiring a specialised circulatory system.
7. Osmosis
Osmosis is another simple process involved in transportation. Organisms like Amoeba and Hydra use such simple processes to move materials.
8. Problem in Large Multicellular Organisms
Large multicellular organisms contain trillions of cells. Using only diffusion and osmosis would take a very long time to transport materials.
9. Development of a Specialised Transport System
To avoid this delay, organisms developed a separate specialised transport system that carries materials much faster and more efficiently throughout the body.

Circulatory System

Circulatory system → specialised system that carries materials rapidly and efficiently throughout the body.

We:

  • Eat → solids
  • Drink → liquids
  • Breathe → gases
⭐ The circulatory system helps transport the materials required by different parts of the body.

3.3 Blood Vessels and Circulation

Girolamo Fabrici

In 1574, Italian doctor Girolamo Fabrici studied veins in the leg. He noticed small valves in veins that allowed blood to flow towards the heart — one-way valves that prevented backflow. Movement of leg muscles helps blood move through veins. Fabrici did not recognise the full importance of his discovery.

William Harvey

William Harvey (1578–1657) was an English doctor. He studied under Fabrici in Italy, dissected human hearts, and studied valves between atria and ventricles. He recognised that they were one-way valves.

Atrium → Valve → Ventricle

During contraction: Ventricle → Artery. The blood cannot flow backwards into the atrium.

Harvey's Experiments

Harvey blocked different veins in animals. Veins bulged on the side of the block away from the heart, showing blood in veins was trying to move towards the heart. In arteries, blood bulged on the heart side of the block, showing arterial blood was moving away from the heart.

Harvey's Conclusion

Heart → Arteries → Body/Lungs → Veins → Heart

Blood therefore circulates.

Pulmonary Circulation: Right ventricle → Lungs → Left atrium
Systemic Circulation: Left ventricle → Aorta → Body → Veins → Right atrium
⭐ Because blood reaches the heart twice during a complete circulation, it is called double circulation.

Harvey's Other Evidence

A belief existed that blood leaving the heart was used up and new blood was continuously produced. Harvey disproved this by measuring blood pumped in one contraction, counting contractions per minute, and calculating that in one hour, the heart pumped blood equal to about three times the weight of a man. The body could not produce and use blood at that rate.

⭐ Therefore, the same blood must circulate repeatedly.

Marcello Malpighi

Marcello Malpighi (1628–1694) used a microscope to observe tiny blood vessels. He studied the thin membranes of bat wings and saw that the smallest arteries and veins were connected by extremely fine vessels. He called them capillaries — from a Latin word meaning hair, because they are extremely thin.

⭐ Discovery of capillaries completed the explanation of blood circulation.
🧠 Scientists Memory Trick: F → H → M
Fabrici → valves  |  Harvey → circulation  |  Malpighi → capillaries

Harvey's Demonstration

  • Tie a tourniquet just above the elbow.
  • Hold a firm support so vessels become prominent.
  • Locate a prominent bluish blood vessel.
  • Apply steady pressure at the end farthest from the elbow.
  • Apply pressure slowly from elbow towards palm; observe changes.
  • Remove pressure.
  • Apply pressure from palm towards elbow.

Inference: The experiment demonstrates the direction of blood flow in veins and the action of their valves.

3.5 The Cardiac Cycle

Human heart begins beating around the 21st day of embryonic development. It beats rhythmically until death. If the heart stops beating, the person dies.

📌 Cardiac Cycle: One contraction + one relaxation of atria and ventricles = one cardiac cycle.

Sequence

  1. Diastole — All four chambers are relaxed.
  2. Filling of atria — Vena cava → Right atrium; Pulmonary veins → Left atrium
  3. Atrial contraction — Atria contract → blood enters ventricles.
  4. Ventricular contraction — Ventricles contract; atrioventricular valves close; first sharp heart sound → LUB; blood moves into Aorta / Pulmonary artery.
  5. Ventricular relaxation — Pressure falls; blood in aorta tries to return; semilunar valves close, preventing backflow into ventricles; second heart sound → DUB; atria fill again; cycle repeats.

Systole and Diastole

Systole → active/contraction phase.
Diastole → resting/relaxation phase.

3.7 Lymphatic System

After sitting without movement during a long overnight journey, footwear may feel tight. In older people, the lower legs may visibly swell. This condition is called oedema.

Why does swelling occur?

As blood passes through capillaries, some fluid and certain materials move out into tissues. These materials must be collected and returned to blood circulation.

Tissue Fluid

Heart → Arteries → Capillaries → Liquid containing nutrients moves into tissues

This liquid is called tissue fluid.

Lymphatic System

Tissue fluid flows through the lymphatic system. Most of it ultimately returns to the bloodstream.

Lymph

Lymph forms a vital link between blood and tissues.

Exchange of Substances

It allows:
Essential substances → Blood → Cells
Excretory products → Cells → Blood

Blood, Lymph, Tissue Fluid and Serum

According to the textbook:

Skeletal Muscles

  • Skeletal muscles contract.
  • Pressure is exerted on lymphatic vessels and veins.
  • Lymph and venous blood are pushed towards the heart.
Skeletal muscle contraction → Pressure on lymphatic vessels & veins → Lymph and venous blood move towards heart

Valves

Valves are present in:

  • Veins
  • Lymphatic vessels

These valves prevent reverse flow.

Valves → Prevent reverse flow → Maintain one-way movement

3.8 Evolution of the Transport (Circulatory) System

Amoeba

The protoplasm of Amoeba shows natural movements called Brownian movements.

These movements help distribute nutrients and oxygen throughout the protoplasm.

Brownian movements → Distribution of nutrients and oxygen → Throughout protoplasm
⭐ Exam Point
Natural movements of protoplasm in Amoeba are called Brownian movements.

Multicellular Animals

  • Intracellular transport still occurs in multicellular animals.
  • Protoplasm is mobile even in human cells.
  • Protoplasmic currents occur even in nerve cells.
  • However, multicellular animals require more complex transport systems.
Increase in body complexity → Greater transport needs → Complex transport system

Open Circulatory System

  • Arthropods developed a pulsatile organ called the heart.

In a closed circulatory system, blood transports materials while remaining within blood vessels.

Examples:

  • Cephalopod molluscs such as Octopus
  • Higher chordates

3.9 Blood Pressure (B.P.)

📌 Definition
Blood pressure → pressure exerted by blood on the walls of blood vessels.
  • Movement of blood through vessels requires pressure.
  • Pressure is highest when the ventricles contract.
  • Ventricular contraction forces blood into arteries.
  • Pressure decreases as the ventricles refill.
  • Blood pressure is usually measured in an artery of the upper arm.

3.11 How Materials Transport Within the Plant

Animals have a large transport system that supplies nutrients and oxygen and removes wastes. Plants also need transportation.

  • Plants obtain water containing minerals from soil through roots.
  • Water absorbed by roots must reach other parts of the plant.
  • Food prepared by leaves must also reach other plant parts.

Vascular Bundles

Transport occurs through vascular bundles containing:

Vascular Tissue Main Function
Xylem Transport of water and minerals
Phloem Transport of food
  • In roots, xylem develops from the periphery towards the centre.
  • In stems, xylem develops from the centre towards the periphery in each vascular bundle.

3.11.1 How is Water Absorbed?

Activity-5 — Absorbing Root Hairs

Materials

Germinated:

  • Bajra seeds OR
  • Mustard seeds
Observation / Procedure
  • Observe seedlings using a hand lens.
  • Fine threads coming from the seed are roots.
  • Roots possess microscopic structures called root hairs.
  • Water enters plants through root hairs.
  • Squash a portion of root hair between a slide and cover slip.
  • Add a drop of water.
  • Observe under a compound microscope.
  • Observe that root-hair walls are thin.

Osmosis

📌 Osmosis plays an important role in the movement of water from the soil into the plant.
Root Hair → Cells → Xylem
  • Every living cell acts as an osmotic system.
  • The cytoplasmic lining acts as the semipermeable membrane.
  • Root hairs grow between soil particles.
  • They are surrounded by moisture.
  • Soil water is an extremely dilute solution of salts.
  • Soil water is more dilute than the cell sap in root hairs.

Movement of Water into Root Hairs

Soil Water → Osmosis → Root-Hair Vacuole
Root Hair → Neighbouring Cells → Cortex → Xylem Vessels
  • Water enters the root-hair vacuole by osmosis.
  • Root-hair contents become diluted.
  • Water then enters the neighbouring cell.
  • The neighbouring cell becomes diluted.
  • Water continues to pass from cell to cell.
  • Finally, it reaches the xylem vessels.

Root Pressure

Because large numbers of root hairs and root cells participate in water absorption, pressure develops in the xylem. This pressure helps force water upward.
📌 Root Pressure → total pressure developed in xylem that helps force water upwards.
⭐ Important: Root pressure is not the main cause of water movement in xylem. It is only one contributing factor.

3.11.2 The Mechanism of Water Movement in Plants

Root pressure gives a push from below, but:

  • It is seldom very high.
  • In some seasons it may be nil.
  • Yet water can reach about 180 metres to the top of a eucalyptus tree.

Transpiration

📌 Transpiration → evaporation of water through leaves.

Water evaporates through:

  • Stomata of leaves
  • Lenticels of stem

Transpiration Pull

  • Leaves transpire.
  • Water evaporates from mesophyll-cell walls into air spaces.
  • A pulling effect develops.
  • Continuous columns of water in xylem are pulled upward.
  • The water column does not break because of continuous molecular attraction.

The textbook compares this property with drinking through a straw.

Relation Between Transpiration and Rainfall

  • Plants may transpire enormous amounts of water.
  • An oak tree may transpire about 900 litres/day.
  • Forests increase the moisture/saturation of air.
  • Nearly saturated air entering a forest may become fully saturated.
  • This can result in rain.
  • Therefore, forest areas often receive more rainfall than nearby areas.

3.11.3 Transport of Mineral Salts

  • Plants need mineral salts for nutrition.
  • These include micro and macro nutrients.
  • Mineral salts are obtained from the soil solution through root hairs.
  • Salts occur as electrically charged ions.

Examples of Mineral Ions

NaCl → Na⁺ + Cl⁻
MgSO₄ → Mg²⁺ + SO₄²⁻
  • Mineral ions are not absorbed simply by diffusion.
  • Their absorption involves the use of energy by the cytoplasm.

Movement of Mineral Ions

Mineral Ions + Water → Xylem Vessels → Growing Regions
  • Mineral ions are transported to growing regions.
  • They are used for plant growth.
  • They may also move laterally from xylem to phloem.
⭐ Mineral salts are natural factors involved in plant growth.

3.12 Transport of Food Materials in Plants

Food such as sugar is synthesised in green parts of the plant, mainly the leaves.

Food must reach:

  • All living cells
  • Actively growing cells
  • Food-storage cells

Leaf Veins

Leaf veins contain:
  • Xylem
  • Phloem
These tissues continue into the stem.
⭐ Experiments show that food is transported through phloem.

Aphid Experiment

  • Phloem sieve tubes are extremely small.
  • Their contents are difficult to analyse directly.
  • Biologists therefore used aphids/greenfly to study food transport.

Aphid Feeding

  • Aphids cluster around young stems.
  • They feed on plant juices.
  • An aphid has a long needle-like organ called a proboscis.
  • The proboscis penetrates up to a phloem sieve tube.

Aphid Experiment — Procedure

  • Allow an aphid to feed on the plant.
  • Kill it while it is feeding.
  • Carefully remove its body.
  • Leave the hollow proboscis inserted into the phloem.
  • Phloem contents are under slight pressure.
  • Fluid slowly comes out in the form of drops.
  • Collect these drops.
  • Analyse the collected fluid.
Result

The fluid contains:

  • Sugars
  • Amino acids
Conclusion

Food materials are transported through phloem.

⭐ Aphid Experiment Conclusion: Food materials are transported through phloem.

Honey Dew

💡 Honey Dew

Aphids absorb large amounts of sugar but cannot assimilate all of it. The excess comes out as a sticky syrup called honey dew.

Therefore, leaves attacked by aphids may feel sticky.

Fig-19: Aphid extracting food material from plant — Labels: Aphid, Proboscis, Phloem.

Ringing Experiment

Ringing Experiment — Procedure

  • Remove a ring of bark from a shoot.
  • Expose the wood.
  • Remove tissues from the centre outward, including the phloem.
Observation after a few days
  • Food accumulates above the ring.
  • Food is absent below the ring.
After more time:
  • The stem becomes thicker above the ring.
  • No growth occurs below the ring.
Conclusion

Phloem conducts food.

Phloem damaged completely → Food cannot reach roots → Roots lose food supply → Tree eventually dies
⭐ Ringing Experiment Conclusion: Phloem conducts food.

Economic Importance

  • Some mammals scratch bark to obtain food stored in phloem.
  • This becomes particularly important during hard winters when food is scarce.
  • Voles, which are rat-like rodents, can damage young saplings at ground level.
  • Rabbits can damage older trees.
  • Foresters may surround new plantations with wire netting to keep rabbits out.
